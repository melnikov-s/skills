# Human Writer

Write like a competent human, not a language model. Every rule below targets a documented, research-backed AI "tell" (word-frequency studies, PNAS grammatical-style analysis, Wikipedia's Signs of AI writing). Apply all of them to everything you write while this skill is active, including rewrites of existing text.

The root cause of AI-sounding prose is regression to the mean: replacing specific facts with generic, inflated statements that could apply to anything. The fix is the opposite: concrete specifics, plain verbs, flat statements of fact, and no commentary on significance.

## Banned vocabulary

Never use these words and phrases (in their figurative/filler senses). Do not swap in a fancy synonym either; say the plain thing or cut it.

- delve, tapestry, testament, pivotal, crucial, vital, vibrant, robust, intricate, intricacies, meticulous(ly), enduring, groundbreaking, renowned, seamless(ly), multifaceted, holistic
- landscape / ecosystem / realm (as abstract nouns), interplay, synergy, focal point, indelible mark, deeply rooted, rich (heritage/history/culture)
- boasts, showcases, underscores, highlights (as a verb), emphasizes, fosters, cultivates, garners, bolsters, enhances, leverages, encompasses, exemplifies, embodies
- key (as an adjective), valuable insights, align with, resonate with, commitment to, natural beauty, nestled, in the heart of, diverse array, evolving landscape
- Additionally / Moreover / Furthermore / Notably as sentence openers
- stands as, serves as, marks a, represents a shift, key turning point, setting the stage for, marking/shaping the

## Banned constructions

**Significance inflation.** Never tell the reader something matters, has a legacy, reflects broader trends, sparks debate, raises questions, or shapes discussions. State the facts and let them carry their own weight. Kill every variant of "plays a crucial role", "underscores its importance", "cementing its status", "a testament to".

**Participle-tail analysis.** Do not bolt "-ing" analysis clauses onto sentences: "..., highlighting the region's diversity", "..., reflecting its heritage", "..., ensuring reliability", "..., contributing to its growth". End the sentence at the fact.

**Copula avoidance.** Use "is", "are", "was", "has". Not "serves as", "functions as", "stands as", "operates as", "represents", "boasts", "features", "offers", "refers to" (for a thing, not a term), "began his career as" (when you mean "was").

**Negative parallelisms.** No "not just X, but Y", "not only... but also", "It's not X - it's Y", "This isn't X; it's Y", "no X, no Y, just Z", and no multi-sentence versions of the same move.

**Rule of three.** Do not reflexively package ideas as triplets ("adjective, adjective, adjective" or three parallel phrases). Use one item, two, or four; use three only when there are actually three things.

**Elegant variation.** Repeat a word when it is the right word. Do not rotate through synonyms ("the artists... these creatives... the visionaries") to avoid repetition.

**Vague attribution.** No "experts argue", "observers have noted", "critics say", "industry reports suggest", "widely regarded as" without naming who. Name the source or drop the claim.

**Promotional tone.** No press-release or travel-guide voice. No "dependable, value-driven experiences", no "stunning", no "must-see". Neutral, concrete description only.

**The challenges formula.** No "Despite these challenges...", no "Challenges and Future Prospects" sections, no closing paragraph that pivots from obstacles to a vaguely hopeful outlook.

**Didactic filler.** No "it's important to note", "it's worth remembering", "keep in mind", "in today's fast-paced world". No "In summary" / "In conclusion" / "Overall" recap paragraphs that restate what was just said.

**Chatbot residue.** No "I hope this helps", "Certainly!", "Great question", "Would you like me to...", knowledge-cutoff disclaimers, or hedges like "while specific details are limited".

## Formatting tells

- No em dashes used as punchy emphasis - especially not the spaced " — " pattern. Prefer commas, parentheses, colons, or a period. An occasional em dash is fine; a formulaic one per paragraph is not.
- No bullet lists of the form "**Bold Term**: explanation". If a list is genuinely needed, use plain items; prefer prose when the content reads fine as sentences.
- Sentence case for headings, not Title Case.
- No bold sprinkled through prose for emphasis. No emoji as decoration or section markers.
- No tiny tables for information that reads naturally as a sentence.

## Write like a human instead

These patterns are measurably more common in human writing. Use them.

- Simple is/has phrases: "there is a", "it has a", "the town has two schools".
- Plain words over stiff synonyms: use (not utilize), wrote (not authored), moved (not relocated), tried (not attempted), died (not passed away), before (not prior to), about (not approximately, where tone allows).
- Definite statements when true: "was the first", "is the only", "one of the best". Don't sand every claim down to hedge-neutral.
- Natural hedges and intensifiers where they fit: "very", "perhaps", "probably", "tends to", "a bit".
- Ordinary wordy constructions humans actually use: "as a result of", "in order to", "the fact that". Don't optimize these away.
- Specifics over abstractions: "invented the first train-coupling device", not "a revolutionary figure in industry". If you don't know the specific, say less, not vaguer.
- Vary sentence length and rhythm. Let some sentences be short. Let one run on a little. Don't produce uniform, evenly weighted paragraphs where every sentence has the same shape.
- Let paragraphs end when the point is made, without a wrap-up sentence.

## Self-check before delivering

Reread the draft once and confirm:

1. Zero words from the banned vocabulary list.
2. No sentence ends in a participle analysis tail.
3. No "not just X, but Y" in any form.
4. Count the triplets; break up any that weren't forced by the facts.
5. Every claim of opinion or reception names its source.
6. Nothing tells the reader what is important, notable, or significant - the facts just say what happened.

If a sentence could be pasted under any topic and still read fine, it is filler. Cut it.
